# 1 INTRODUCTION

We would like to thank you for your willingness to act as a final project supervisor for the TechMBA Professional Masters programme at Amsterdam Tech. The final project and its assessment are the last hurdles for students to complete their Masters. With the project, the students have to demonstrate that they have mastered the skills and knowledge associated with the degree.

These guidelines give an overview of the Professional Masters programme, the supervision process and your responsibilities as a supervisor. They also provide you with the tools to execute your responsibilities as a supervisor. It will empower you to offer guidance both to yourself as well as the student in the various stages of the process of the final project. They will clarify the conditions to which the students should comply and what they can expect from you. Please take some time to read this document as it contains vital information that will enable you to succeed in your role as supervisor. It is also advisable for you to have a look at the most recent Teaching and Examination Regulations (TER). The TER constitutes the ultimate resource on the regulations and processes guiding this Professional Masters programme.

We see the final project as a journey in which all the course’s learning points are revisited and paid homage to. The supervisors are the guides who ensure all the right spots are made part of the tour and actually visited by the student. The students are to put the tour’s itinerary together and carefully consider every designated point along the way by showing their acquired mastership.

# 2 LEARNING GOALS AND LEARNING OUTCOMES

To understand the learning goals and learning outcomes of the final project and its thesis, it is important to be aware of the mission, values and philosophy of Amsterdam Tech and its TechMBA Professional Masters programme.

AmTech Mission, Values and Educational Philosophy

Amsterdam Tech was founded in 2016 to bridge the skills gap that businesses currently face. We do this by engaging with employers and developing talents to give them the skills they need now. Our aim is simple in design but ambitious in scope – to invest in talent for the fourth industrial revolution. We are guided by five core values at Amsterdam Tech:0

Human Potential: We truly believe in people’s potential to develop when the right learning environment is created.

Collaboration: We know that every individual brings a unique perspective to the table and positive impact is made possible through collaboration.

Community Learning: Humans are social beings; therefore, learning is most effective when it happens in a community.

Transparency: Being open helps us build trust and integrity among the AmTech’s community members.

Innovation: We constantly explore tools and processes to make learning more relevant in the digital age.

Our approach and methodology is rooted in a core activity – the full-stack education programme. By organizing and facilitating these programmes, we offer an intensive learning experience: multidisciplinary teams help our students with mentoring and in-depth collaboration with the business community. Our degree programmes are in line with the latest developments in digital technology – developing optimum capability to devise innovative solutions. All programmes are digital-related. Amsterdam Tech is not tied to a traditional classroom approach. Instead, we focus on domain mastery, giving our students the knowledge they need in the workplace, and on leadership mastery, giving them the skills they need to work in teams and communicate effectively.

Professional Mastery[1](#footnote1)

Professional masters work in a professionally oriented context on complex issues related to their professional practice. They do this based on solid theoretical knowledge and research-based methods and attitude. They act professionally on the basis of ethical and moral consciousness and are autonomous and reflective. They have progressed to an advanced level and demonstrate mastery in their discipline. They work independently and collaborate interprofessionally in various networks and contribute to knowledge creation and innovation.

equip participants with the knowledge and skills to strategically integrate AI technologies into business strategies;

develop agile leaders who can harness the power of emerging technologies to adapt to and lead change in organisations;

# 3\. TECHMBA PROFESSIONAL MASTERS

Summative Assessment system

The programme’s summative system is set out in four parts. Each part consists of a variety of interim- assessments, which together are weighted and ultimately result in a final qualification based on a numeric evaluation score (out of 100) and a caesura of 60 to pass the programme’s certifications.

Transform your Business Certification: The final score of a learner is calculated based on 50% module projects, 30% weekly assignments and 10% self-reflective reports, 10% knowledge checks.

Lead Your Business Certification:The final score of a learner is calculated based on 50% module projects, 30% weekly assignments and 10% self-reflective reports, 10% knowledge checks.

Innovate and Grow Your Business Certification: The final score of a learner is calculated based on 50% module projects, 30% weekly assignments and 10% self-reflective reports, 10% knowledge checks.

Transformative Leadership Certification: The final score of a learner is calculated based on 50% module projects, 30% weekly assignments and 10% self-reflective reports, 10% knowledge checks.

Final Project: The Final Project is an obligatory component. For participants to graduate, individually, they must submit an original piece of work in which they are required to showcase all the knowledge and competencies they acquired during the programme.

The Final Project (21,5 ECTS)

The outcome of the final project at AmTech programmes is intended to be a submission of (1) a work-based project plan along with (2) a written report, (3) a video presentation and a (4) Q\&A live session that will satisfy the assessment criteria. It is aimed to ensure that a learner progresses towards their project with high expectations of a successful outcome and thus, planning is a crucial part of the process. Overall the final project is a great opportunity for learners to exercise both practitioner research and leadership skills. Note that the academic board examines a learner’s overall profile and finalizes the coursework grades prior to the project module to determine whether they can proceed with their final project. They must achieve at least an average of 60 on the previous certificates - equivalent to proficiency level – to be able to proceed with the final project module.

Objectives in the Final Project

The final project is the last part of your programme and the successful completion of your professional master. It is a chance for you to put all the competencies you learned - both technology and leadership - into practice. The outcome is intended to be a submission of a work-based project along with a written report, a recorded presentation and a live Q\&A session. The principal question to ask is “How can I improve my practice?” With this question in mind, this part of the programme offers a practical and structured way of applying action research in a workplace, organisation or independently.

The project you undertake may be driven by an organisational need or aim for a social good. It may therefore be designed in relation to several needs:

\- to solve a work-based problem within an organisation;

\- to review an existing aspect of an organisational practice;

\- to launch a digital transformation strategy;

\- to enquire into a particular area with a view to developing it;

\- to introduce a new methodology for doing something in a different way;

\- to introduce a solution to a social problem.

Methodology

In order to progress towards a successful outcome of your project, we employ a method called Action Research. As a research methodology which develops knowledge through being and doing. In other words, it is grounded in your own lived experience aiming to develop a living theory of practice development. You will do that by asking a fundamental but profound question: “How can I improve my practice?” As an action researcher, you will be able to create an educational, participative and transformational perspective in your own learning, in the learning of others and in the learning of the social formation in which you live and work.

“Action research is a situated activity that locates the observer in the world. \[It\] consists of a set of interpretive, material practices that make the world visible. These practices transform the world. They turn the world into a series of representations, including field notes, interviews, conversations, photographs, recordings, and memos to the self. This means that qualitative researchers study things in their natural settings, attempting to make sense of or interpret phenomena in terms of the meanings people bring to them.”

Action research: How does it work?   
This research methodology proposes an iterative research cycle as below.

Constructing What is my inquiry, and why does it matter?

Planning action With whom, with what, and how will I inquire?

Taking action What is the evidence of my data?

Evaluating action To what extent are the outcomes in line with my intention, my plan and my data? What more needs to happen?

Your project will be conducted in two cycles. Once you complete your project activities in cycle 1, you will review your previous actions, reflect on them and improve them in cycle 2.

Action Research Basic Recipe

Articulate the action research question : How can I…? …in relation to these issues that present for me and others in this situation, within this context and culture, for whom it matters because…

Develop a view of your participants in the project : …of those who affect or are affected by your action research. Who needs to be involved? Who has influence in the system?

Co-design a mutual, participative process : …that is clear about the degree of participation appropriate at each stage from articulating the research question to evaluating the results

Consider what evidence will be needed : …as quantitative data and qualitative experiences to provide evidence of change.

Bring new information to the group and facilitate dialogue(s)on outcomes in relation to the intended changes.

Co-develop an action plan : …with those who are able to make the research happen

Reflect together on your learning : ..to evaluate the personal, collective and systemic development experiences from participating in the action research

# 4\. SUPERVISING THE PROJECT

The Cycles and Steps

The action research practice starts within the leadership and action research (LAR) modules. This phase of the programme integrates action research methodology with leadership practice. At the start of the Final Project phase, learners will have constructed their project idea, planned their actions, presented their project plan and got it approved by the organization and their supervisor. In the following section, you will find more information as to each project step. The project supervisor is expected to evaluate progress at the completion of each step so learners can be made aware of any shortcomings before they move further in their project.

Pre-step: Understand context & purpose.

What is the rationale for action? You start thinking about why this project is necessary or desirable (especially also for you as an individual), and start to make sense of the economic, social or technical forces driving the need for action.

What is the rationale for research? This involves asking how action research is an appropriate methodology to adopt and what contribution it is expected to make.

Cycle 1, Step 1: Exploring and Constructing the Project Proposal

This is the start of defining the project problem that you want to have an inquiry about and bring a solution to, within your context, by combining your action research and TechMBA mindset. The primary question asked at this stage is “What do I want to achieve and/or improve?”. Besides defining your problem, it is a stage when you need to compose a project proposal. This should include a literature review of different or partial approaches to solve the same problem. Besides, some preliminary desktop research that you have done on the topic can be integrated. The proposal should make you think through exactly what it is that you are going to do, and reflect on the leadership questions that need to be addressed at this stage.

Cycle 1, Step 2: Planning Action

Building on your project proposal, you will delve into the specifics of strategic planning while composing your project plan. Critical questions to explore include:

The context in which you work and how it may influence the project;

The challenges or obstacles you might face during the project lifetime;

Your role as an insider researcher;

How are you improving your leadership skills and problem solving capabilities?

What are you learning about yourself, the difference you offer and the impact you make?

What do you want to achieve with this? What will be the tangible outcomes / end product? This includes the practical aspects of what you want to see at the end.

What strategies and frameworks in technology and leadership will guide your project's implementation?

What do other people say about your research problem? How does existing research and knowledge inform your project's strategic focus? This requires an exploration and reading of supporting literature, desktop research, sector, professional, organizational or any other relevant source, e.g. government policies.

Where is the information? What sort of data will you be collecting and how? Is this the best way? Data may come from various sources. What are ethical and legal considerations related to gathering, storing and using information or accessing data?

What resources do you need? Time? People? Materials? Identification of what work and home resources are available and who is available to support or inform you.

What methods do you need to use to answer the question? How will you analyze the data?

What will your end product / solution look like? This needs planning from the earliest stages. What do you envision the end product to look like?

What will you do, when and for how long? What’s the timescale and how is it organized? What is the project's timeline and milestone plan?

What are your existing skills and what new skills do you need to accomplish the project outcomes? You should realistically consider the skills you already have as well as how much you need to stretch your existing skill set within the scope of this project and end product.

How will you evaluate your end product / solution? How will you collect user feedback for your solution? Is it possible to collect feedback from the users of the end product?

Who are the key stakeholders and how will they be involved?

How will existing skills be utilised and new ones acquired to meet strategic objectives?

What methods will ensure the validation of the project's strategic approach?

Cycle 1, Step 3: Taking Action

By this stage, your project concept is well-defined, and you will start putting your strategic plan into action. It is time to create a prototype of your end product or solution. The following question will guide this process: does the prototype provide you with what you thought of and planned earlier? Learning activities at this stage include:

Prototyping the product/solution;

Considering how leadership skills and problem solving capabilities are improving and what is being learnt about oneself, the difference one is offering and the impact one is making.

Cycle 1, Step 4: Evaluating Action

This phase involves evaluating the impact of your strategic actions. You will assess the outcomes in terms of strategic alignment and organisational change, and are now ready to go to people or users to gather feedback for iterations & improvements regarding your actions and prototype. This stage is important in terms of looking to see if everything worked so far, how it worked or did not work. At this stage some of the questions are:

How can reliable feedback best be collected?

What are the outcomes of the first cycle of planning and action?

How do the outcomes match with the previously constructed objectives?

How do the outcomes show that the actions were implemented (in)appropriately?

How do the outcomes show that the appropriate people were involved/not involved?

How does the overall evaluation of the outcomes point to what to plan to do next?

How are you improving your leadership skills and problem solving capabilities?

What are you learning about yourself, the difference you offer and the impact you make?

How do the outcomes align with strategic objectives?

How do the results reflect the effectiveness of the implemented strategies?

Were the right stakeholders involved?

What insights do the evaluation provide for the next steps?

Cycle 2, Step 1: Constructing the End Product and Refining Organisational Strategies

In Cycle 2, you review and improve your previous research actions while at the same time you start constructing or composing your end product / solution based on learnings and reflections. Simultaneously, you start scaling your strategies to achieve greater organisational change. Key reflection points include:

Constructing / composing your end product or solution;

Improving the project question, project design or methodology. How can the original strategies be refined for better outcomes? Are there new questions or objectives that have emerged?

Reflection on leadership style, performance and progress questions that need to be addressed at this stage;

Reflection on what you are learning about yourself, the difference you are offering and the impact you are having.

What have you learned from your previous actions and how will it guide further changes?

Cycle 2, Step 2: Reviewing the End Product and Iterating for Organisational Impact

In this stage, the focus is on refining your organisational strategies based on feedback and insights from relevant stakeholders. Also, you will need to review the end product / solution through feedback from relevant parties. Further reflection questions might include:

How can you obtain reliable feedback on the product?

Do your original strategic plans still align with organisational goals?

Is there a need to adapt the strategies or project plan? Is there anything new that needs to be integrated into the project plan in terms of the methodology or end product, etc?

Does the original project plan still work? Do previously planned actions need to be revised? Are there any new actions that need to be taken? Are there new actions or approaches that will drive more significant change?

How are leadership skills and problem solving capabilities being improved?

What lessons are you picking up about yourself, the difference you are offering and the impact you are having.

Cycle 2, Step 3: Finalizing the End Product and Presenting for Organisational Change

At this stage, you will finalise your end product and your strategic actions, implement the necessary changes, and present the solutions to your organisation. This includes completing your project report, reflective account, and conclusions. Further reflection questions might include:

How do you evaluate Cycle 1 and 2 overall? What worked well, what did not?

What did you learn? And how do these new learnings affect your leadership and action researcher practices?

What impact, difference or change has been achieved both in the roles as advisor, researcher, leader considering the three different audiences within action research?

Final Report Presentations:

This takes place only after the written or video version of your project report is found satisfactory by the two internal examiners (and, if necessary, also by an external examiner). Then you need to submit an oral presentation on the final project’s course from start to finish focussing on both Tech and LAR components. This presentation has to be in the form of a video recording.

In the video, you can make use of a PowerPoint or other presentation tool, but you need to be on screen as a presenter. The video should be between 15-30 minutes in duration and cover the final project in sufficient detail for an outsider to feel they have been well-introduced to the project, and for a supervisor to recognize salient learning points that you experienced during the project. The focus of your video presentation be will be on:

Motivating the project and its objectives in driving organisational change;

Clearly defining the strategic problem or question addressed;

Outlining your methodology and approach to driving change;

Presenting the outcomes achieved through the strategic approach;

Sharing insights from each inquiry cycle and step;

Concluding with the implications of the change, potential further research, and the lasting impact on the organisation..

One week after submitting the video, you receive an invitation to a VIVA-session where the final project supervisors/examiners ask questions that challenge you to recount why you made certain decisions during the project and to expand on the possible consequences of your project from both Tech and LAR perspectives.

Assessment of your Final Project

15% Project Plan

30% Action Research Methodology

40% Technology Focus

15% Video Presentation & QA session

# 5\. SUPERVISION PROCESS

Before students formally begin with their final project, they must first successfully complete the three separate programme parts. During the final LAR modules, learners will be asked to develop their Project Proposal and Project Plan. As you have read earlier, the Proposal outlines the initial ideas of the final assessment. After receiving feedback on the initial proposal, the students will then write their Project Plan. This is a complete research proposal, indicating the main goals and the research methods and data that will be used.

Role of the Supervisor

Allocation of the supervisor occurs after submission of the Project Proposal and while students are working on their Project Plan. While composing the initial project proposal, students are of course already supported by the different Tech and LAR faculty members. AmTech aims to find a perfect match between a student and supervisor in terms of business, theoretical, methodological, and empirical interests and expertise. Supervisors are meant to guide students, not do the work for them. This means their role is to support the final project process by checking in on students and asking probing questions, not to co-write the final project report for them. Although we would all like students to graduate with high grades, this is dependent on the ability and the dedication of each student.

The supervisor’s job is to bring the best out of the student. However, at the same time, supervisors need to be honest and clear in their feedback and communication with the student, especially as normally the supervisor will also act as the final examiner and as such award certain grades. Our experience is that a supervisor who has not been clear enough usually becomes an examiner who can’t be honest enough. If you as a supervisor or as an examiner are ever in doubt about what to do in the process or on how to grade a student, please know that there are always faculty members available in the background ready to help you out with a four-eye check.

We expect the Tech supervisor to meet with students several times. This would be for the initial meeting to discuss the final project and then for different milestones. However, this is a minimum requirement and more than one meeting per milestone is absolutely considered acceptable. Students also have to make appointments to discuss their project with an LAR faculty member who acts as supporting supervisor to receive extra feedback about their progress in the areas of Leadership and Action Research.

Communication

During the student’s progression and supervisors feedback cycles, both the student as well as (and especially) the supervisors should be aware of the fact that all communication related to the final project will use the channels facilitated by AmTech. Day to day communication will happen via Slack and or AmTech-email. Formal submission and feedback will take place via Canvas).

Review of the Project Plan

The first meeting with the student is to review the draft Project Plan which should be based on the already approved Project Proposal. The supervisor needs to establish if and how the proposed project needs to be further fine-tuned. Are the objectives clear, do they make sense, are they of value? Is the project feasible? Does the student have access to the required information and data? Is the student able to carry out the task? Furthermore, the planning needs to be reviewed. Is it feasible? Adjustments should be made to fit both the student and the supervisor’s schedule. The score that is assigned to the Project Plan accounts for the first 15% of the Final Project’s end grade. Thus, this score should reflect the supervisor’s very objective opinion of the project as envisioned by the student and the depth in which the student describes the different components of the plan which are further explained in Appendix V. The grade should not reflect the supervisor’s enthusiasm for the project, but the student’s ability to make a convincing start at a Masters level (\!\!) on their final project journey.

Planning

The final project is a huge undertaking for the student and it requires great discipline and motivation to keep focused. A clear planning will help to keep the project moving along. The planning is not fixed in stone and can be adjusted, although the deadline set in the programme schedule remains standing. In case a student needs to request a deadline extension, the procedure described in TER art.3.5 needs to be followed. Extension requests (only valid once with a maximum six month duration) should be done at least two weeks before the deadline expires. In this case it helps enormously if the supervisor has kept track of the student’s progress with short comments on how well (or badly) the thesis is progressing during the supervisory process.

To help with the planning and with clarity on the process and responsibilities, we require students to sign a Final Project Agreement Form (Appendix III). Also, students must write down a date when they expect to complete different project milestones in their project plan.

Academic Integrity & the use of advanced technologies

Starting with the planning stage, students are expected to follow Amsterdam Tech’s institutional [policy and the guidelines on the use of Advanced Technology Tools,](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1Jh13YRH-R6sOp7sXAZ9nR6nou5BLd-rHgtdvnju1IEw/edit?tab=t.0) which is grounded in disclosure, accountability, and reflection. During the final project, this is operationalised through a structured approach in which students plan, track, and make their AI use visible throughout the process.

At the planning stage, students define their intended use of AI tools in the Final Project Agreement (AI Usage Planning), including purpose and expected level of use. During supervision, this is updated iteratively to reflect actual use.

At submission, students are required to explicitly document their AI use in the report through an AI Use Statement and a reflective account, and must be able to demonstrate that their analysis, interpretation, coding, modelling, and conclusions remain their own. Supervisors should therefore not only check whether AI was used, but whether its use remained accountable, particularly in high-risk areas such as analysis, reasoning, and decision-making, where independent intellectual ownership must be clearly demonstrated.

Feedback

It is essential to provide students with clear feedback at every step. In short, it is the supervisor’s duty to help the student to bring a project to a higher level than where it would have been if the student had been unsupervised. Supervisors tasks are as follows:

To question student’s Tech and LAR choices and thus force them to re-evaluate them;

To provide focus and feedback;

To discuss the planning;

To advise on the choice of literature;

To advise on the research methods;

To ensure the paper meets academic standards;

To sign off on the final version of the final project report before it gets submitted.

Submission

As supervisor, you need to check with the programme manager for the exact submission date. Projects may only be submitted following the approval of the supervisor. Usually, the supervisor will also be one of the internal or external final project examiners, and in this case it is extremely important that there is a very conscious awareness that you are NOT grading the report in a cordial - supervisor relationship, but in a totally separate graduate candidate - examiner relationship. This means that the score should be very carefully evaluated; it should NOT be a reflection of the personal connection between supervisor and student, but ONLY an academic evaluation of the thesis and its value towards the field.

Supervision Challenges and How to Prevent or Handle Them

Supervisors may face several challenges. Below we list some of those challenges and provide suggestions to handle them.

Dormant students: These are students who do not maintain (any) contact with their supervisor. These students never send emails, rarely meet with their supervisor and/or claim to be too busy to concentrate fully on their final project. If attempts to stimulate students to work on their project fail, please contact the programme manager.

Non-performing and/or overly dependent students: These are students that are continuously asking their supervisor what they should do even after receiving helpful comments that should allow them to advance their paper. They lack any self-management skills and just do not seem to know what they need to do to make appropriate progress with their project even after putting in significant effort. As above, seek contact with the programme manager.

Academic dishonesty: It can happen that the supervisor suspects a case of academic dishonesty (such as fraud, forgery, academic outsourcing or plagiarism). As above, seek contact with the programme manager who can explain the formal procedure.

Uncertainty about a grade in the role of examiner: in case you feel any doubt on how to grade a student, please contact the programme manager who will put you in touch with another content specialist so that you can discuss this problem with an equal and come to a more informed decision which you both feel comfortable with and are able to explain if needed;

Supervisors and students are asked to sign a “contract” at the start of the process which amongst others includes the student’s agreement to behave in a proactive manner (appendix III-A).

## Appendix VI: Evaluation Process of the Final Project

Submission Check & Initial Review

The Programme Manager receives the Final Project Report with all appendices in a Google document and checks if the requirements have been met (required format, all submissions done, deadlines met). The Programme Manager keeps track of all steps and their dates in a simple table.

Passing to Examiners

Within one week the Programme Manager officially passes the report on to two internal examiners (DS and LAR) adding any remarks on missing submissions and criteria items (e.g. not enough words or not the right format).

Initial Review & Preliminary Decision

Within two weeks upon receiving the final report, both Internal Examiners review the report and write their feedback. Within those same two weeks, the Internal Examiners discuss whether the results of the Project thus far deserve a preliminary Pass or Fail.

Preliminary Feedback to Student

The Internal Examiners pass on their opinion with the feedback to the Programme Manager who shares this with the student on Canvas. The student then has a maximum of four weeks to submit his/her final report again (this presents the opportunity to improve on their level of Mastery).

Final Review of Revised Report

Within two weeks upon receiving the revised final report, the Internal Examiners review and grade[2](#footnote2) the (revised) project report together and discuss whether the results of the Project deserve a final Pass or Fail. The results are passed on to the Programme Manager.

External Examiner Review (If Needed)

In case the Internal Examiners do not agree in their assessment on Pass or Fail, within two weeks the Programme Manager will appoint an External Examiner, who then, within two weeks upon receiving the (revised) final report, reviews the project against the criteria and shares his/her feedback with the Internal Examiners. If the External Examiner decides on a Fail the candidate will officially fail on their Final Project and not be able to graduate from the programme. If the External Examiner decides on a Pass, the candidate will be allowed to start preparing for the oral presentation of his/her final project findings to the Internal Examiners.

Completion of Grading Grid & Final Feedback

All feedback collates in a filled out grading grid by internal (and possibly external) examiners which is forwarded to the student by the Programme Manager within one week after the Examiners have reported their Pass/Fail decision on the report.

Green Light for Presentation Video

The final project video presentation to examiners can only be submitted after the in the grading grid suggested reviews and recommendations are taken on board by the candidate. The Programme Manager checks this with the candidate and then gives the green light to start making the final project presentation video.

Submission and Grading of Final Presentation Video

The candidate submits their final project presentation video within three weeks of receiving the green light and will upon submission receive an invitation for the VIVA q\&a session within another two weeks. The Internal Examiners view and grade the final project presentation video before the q\&a session takes place.

Final Assessment Announcement

The final result is made and announced to the candidate within 30 minutes after they complete their answering the questions from the panel. The official scoring scheme is: Pass with distinction: 87+, Good Pass: 74-86, Pass: 60-73, Fail: 0-59.

## Appendix VIII: Final Project Grading Grid

Instructions for grading

In the process, there are several moments built in for formal feedback to the student and there are two grading moments:

The student can use the feedback moments to improve on their level of TF and LAR Mastery. The feedback will, at different moments in the process, be given in the form of oral and written remarks by the TF supervisor, an LAR faculty member and the internal examiners;

The first formal grading takes place upon reception of the second (and thus final) submission of the written report. At that time, Internal Examiner 1 and 2 review the Project after improvement, grade the different categories and discuss whether they think that the results of the Project and the written report deserves a Pass or Fail from their perspective. In case Internal Examiner 1 and 2 do not agree in their assessment on Pass or Fail, an External Examiner will be appointed. If the External Examiner decides on a Fail the candidate will officially fail on their Final Project and not be able to graduate from the programme. If the External Examiner decides on a Pass, the candidate will be allowed to start preparing for the video presentation of his/her final project findings and the Q\&A-session with the Internal Examiners.

The second formal grading takes place after the completion of the Q\&A-session when the Internal Examiners discuss their scores for the video presentation and the Q\&A-session. That is also the moment when the Programme Manager can calculate the final score on the Final Project and announce a total Pass/Fail decision. The final passing score is set at minimum 60 for the entire project and a minimum score of 60 on the different category sections in the grading grid..

Graders are expected to grade each sub-category in the grading grid separately. On the Tech Focus components, a criterion can be given ‘NA’ if it did not and did not need to play a role in this specific final project.

Graders should be aware that the grades for the written final report are shared with the candidate before the oral presentation. If they are not satisfied with content or depth of the research itself or of the written report, this should be clear from the awarded grades and the feedback on the report (both during the final project process and at the completion).

The graders should be very aware that for the video presentation and the Q\&A-session, the only things they are grading is the quality of the presentation and the quality of the responses during the q\&a session, and not the content or depth of the research itself or of the written report as these have already been formally evaluated and graded.
