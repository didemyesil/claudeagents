# Introduction

The objective of the Assessment Policy is to offer a science-based framework which allows to define guidelines and directions to assure that assessment is of sufficient quality and is directed to all parties involved in designing, executing, appraising, processing and evaluating assessments in a transparent, reliable and valid manner. This document starts out with a generic section, section A, which applies to all programmes at Team Academy and serves as a guideline to the following section, the B section. The purpose of the A section is to clarify the various drivers to the assessment policy and indicate the direction on how to implement this into the operational processes of the programme, thus embedding and assuring quality in assessments. This B section is more programme-specific and applies directly to the specific educational programmes.

## Vision on Learning & Assessment

As it is Team Academy’s key objective to deliver qualified professionals to the work field, it has adopted competency-based learning and assessment for all of its programmes, and for the first time it is starting to adopt programmatic assessment for its professional Master's programme. The institute aims at preparing its learners to become professionally competent and self-directed in their prospective fields of work, i.e. with a solid and state-of-the-art knowledge base, well-skilled and with the right professional attitude. The intended learning outcomes of each programme are stated in terms of competencies, that have level-wise been aligned with the European Qualification Framework (Dublin Descriptors) and the Dutch Qualification Framework (NLQF), and have been validated by representatives of the relevant field of work, thus ensuring that Team Academy graduates are equipped with those competencies that tie in closely with the demands of the professional field.

With its education, Team Academy shares a promise with its learners and their (future) employers to develop valuable skills and competencies. In order to live up to this promise, Team Academy envisions a system of assessment that in an objective manner establishes if a learner is living up to the standards that are described in the programme outcomes. Even more important, the system supports the learners in their learning journey, aimed at both the time in the programme as well as for their future steps. In this process, quality has a central position. In a cyclical manner, reliability, validity, transparency and inclusiveness of assessment are evaluated and improved. A core of educators and staff is acting as a driving force which seeks to establish a spillover quality push towards and from external expertise from both the professional field of the programme as well as educational professionals. In that way, the vision on assessment can best be described as: ‘Assessment at Team Academy is the fulfilment of the promise of competency based learning and the start of future continual learning and growth of each member of its community’.

On Programmatic Assessment

Traditional assessment methods, such as one-time exams or rigid grading systems, often fail to capture the full scope of a student's competencies. Thus, in line with its competency-based approach, Team Academy embraces programmatic assessment in the professional master's programme, which is intended to improve learning and progress through various data points at different stake-levels and rich feedback mechanism.

## Regulatory, Societal and Academic Insights Into Assessment

Roughly there are three areas of relevance into the assessment system: Regulatory, Societal and Academic.

Regulatory

In the regulatory domain there has been a changing approach towards the judgement in accreditation in the Netherlands. The quality demands towards testing have become more important, resulting in a focus on the system of assessment and the quality of the final products of learners. With that, a focus on the continuous development and securing (borging) of quality within the university’s was emphasized as a point of attention[1](#footnote1). Together with a more structured focus of the accreditation panels[2](#footnote2) on securing of the accreditation quality, a more uniform Dutch-Flemish view on assessment quality is coming into existence.

Societal

Over the past decade, the focus within higher professional education is also shifting towards more applied forms of education, and as a result to make use of authentic professional products in the assessment process, eventually resulting in the need to allow new or non-traditional assessment procedures.

Together with the ongoing integration of advanced technology in society, and success in education, conflicts with the objective assessment of the learner qualities are putting further strains on the assessment domain. In this context, programmatic assessment starts to emerge as a sustainable solution by distributing assessment across time, tools, and contexts, supporting fairer, more authentic, and evidence-based evaluation of learner development.

Also, from the learner perspective, the complexity is rising towards the Educational assessment. In 2023 a report of the Inspectorate on selection in higher professional education pointed out that a more transparent selection system is needed to increase equal opportunities. Even though this selection procedure is applicable on learner entry, it crosses the assessment domain of the education and requires attention from policy & programme managers.

Academic

In order to create structure into the previous tendencies, the quality of Assessment is seen at a holistic level. This means that we rely on the latest scientific insights to help safeguard the quality. This has many different layers and to be able to embed this, we embrace the test web concept. The test web forms the theoretical foundation for the “Testing Tested” methodology which aims to analyse, improve and safeguard the quality of testing. This methodology emphasises that testing competence is part of teacher competence and that teaching and testing are inextricably linked.

This methodology makes it possible to approach education from a vision on an educational level working towards a better control of analysing, improving and safeguarding a (sustainable) quality of testing. ## Roles in the process

Within Team Academy, the people involved in the assessment process are (1) The Curriculum Team composed of the programme management, learning design and development, and faculty members who actively take part in the design and implementation of the assessment, (2) learners who are assessed and assess each other through peer-reviews where relevant and (3) a programme’s examination board, who exercises its legal tasks[5](#footnote5). The Examination Board assures that the assessment method(s) that were designed and executed by the curriculum development team are in line with the programme’s rules & regulations, safeguard the continual proper deployment of assessment methods and tools, and check if graduates have fulfilled all requirements of the programme and, if so, endorse the award of the degree. In its control function, the exam board also has an advisory role to define guidelines and principles relating to the implementation of assessment policy and the exam regulations as defined in the programme’s specific TER.

## Generic outline of the process

Team Academy adheres to the principle of the assessment cycle, and this typically involves the following high-level steps:

Design: Define competencies, learning outcomes and design assessments that align with those outcomes.

Development: Create the actual assessment tasks, such as assignments or projects.

Administration: Implement and administer the assessments to learners.

Assessment: Evaluate learner performance using predetermined criteria or rubrics. This may also include peer reviews / scores and feedback.

Analysis: Review the assessment results for trends, validity, and reliability of the assessments.

Feedback & Feedforward: Provide learners with feedback and feedforward to promote learning and improvement.

Revision: Based on analysis and feedback, make adjustments to the assessments for future use to improve their effectiveness and alignment with learning outcomes.

These steps are cyclical and iterative, promoting continuous improvement in the assessment process. For each of the steps, there are some generic starting points.

## Generic standards for the institute

### The assessment cycle

In any programme, the steps of the assessment cycle should encompass several generic aspects that ensure consistency, fairness, and quality. The key aspects for each programme per step are outlined below and are to be reflected in the programme-specific rules and regulations:

Design (Responsible; Curriculum Team):

Alignment with Learning Outcomes: Ensure assessments are directly tied to the intended learning outcomes. To that extent, each programme will have an overview of modules and associated summative/high stake assessments which are aligned with the programme outcomes.

Diversity of Assessment Methods: Each programme uses a variety of both formative and summative assessment types (e.g., projects, presentations, reflective accounts, written or video formats) to accommodate different learning preferences and skills.

Inclusivity: Design assessments that are accessible to all learners, including those with disabilities or special needs.

Development (Responsible; Curriculum Team):

Clarity of Instructions: Provide clear and unambiguous instructions for assessment tasks. For the designers (e.g assessors) of the assessment, this implies the timelines for design & grading, task quality, grading instructions (caesura), and relevant quality criteria, such as the use of peer-checking (four-eyeing) and substantive feedback and forward and clear instructions for the candidates.

Validity: Ensure that the assessment tasks measure what they are intended to measure. To that extent, each programme at least uses the principle of four-eyeing, where a peer assessor reviews the assessment.

Reliability: Create assessments that yield consistent results when administered under similar conditions.

Administering (Responsible; Assessors, supported by Programme Manager):

Standardised Procedures: The programme applies and manages consistent rules and procedures for administering assessments across different groups.

Security and Integrity: To safeguard against cheating or other forms of academic dishonesty during the assessment process, sufficient checks are made.

Fair Timing and Resources: Students are provided with adequate time and resources to complete the assessments.

Assessment (Responsible; Assessor):

Transparency in Criteria: Use clear evaluation rubrics or marking schemes that are communicated to learners beforehand.

Transparency in Caesura & Conversion tables: The caesura -the minimal score a learner should achieve for a pass/sufficient level- as well as the caesura method are chosen and communicated prior to the start of a course. Conversion tables -which define the number of points required for a certain grade- are mandatory and should indicate whether fixed limit values are used or whether correction factors are applied[6](#footnote6).

Objectivity: Strive for fairness and impartiality in the grading process.

Timeliness: Provide grades within a reasonable time frame after the assessment is completed.

Analysis (Responsible; Curriculum Team):

Data Collection: Gather data on learner performance to evaluate the effectiveness of the assessment.

Assessment Validity: Check whether the assessment accurately reflects learner learning and whether it meets learning outcomes.

Reliability Checks: Ensure consistency across different assessors or groups by means of peer reviews or calibrations.

Psychometric Analysis: The use of psychometrics in establishing quality assessment.

Feedback & Feedforward (Responsible; Assessors):

Constructive: Assessments offer specific, actionable feedback and feedforward that help learners understand their performance and how they can improve.

Formative / Low-stake assessment: Assessments, which do not count toward the mastery scores, are offered within a course as a means of formative action, using feedforward to enhance the competency of a student[7](#footnote7)

Timely: Provide feedback promptly, so it contributes to the learning process.

Balanced: Highlight both strengths and areas for improvement to support learner growth.

Reflective: Feedback and feedforward are a reciprocal process, allowing all learners and assessors to improve their competencies.

Revision (Responsible; Curriculum Team):

Continuous Improvement: Use insights from the analysis and feedback stages to revise and improve the assessment tools and processes.

Stakeholder Involvement: Involve learners, faculty, and external experts (if applicable) in the review process to ensure comprehensive evaluation.

Alignment with Evolving Standards: Update assessments to reflect changes in industry standards, professional practices, or academic expectations.

### Use of Technological Support

#### Purpose and Guidelines for Technical Aids for Students

With the advancement of technology, the use of technical aids in creating and evaluating exams requires some direction. In certain assessments, the use of technical aids is permitted or even required to appropriately assess the learning outcomes. Technical aids include, but are not limited to, calculators, laptops, software applications, simulation programmes, and other technological means such as AI-assisted tools that support learners. The use of these aids must comply with the following guidelines to ensure the integrity of the assessment:

Permitted Aids: The assessment instructions will clearly specify which technical aids are allowed during the assessment. Examples may include: graphing calculators, specific software (such as Excel or statistical programmes), and online tools. Only the explicitly permitted aids may be used.

Communication with Students: Students will be informed at least two weeks prior to the assessment about the allowed technical aids. This information will be provided through the programme handbooks or specific assessment instructions. Additionally, the use of these aids will be practised in class or during practical sessions.

Student Responsibility: Students are responsible for bringing and ensuring the proper functioning of their own technical aids. The university will provide the necessary resources when university-specific software is required.

Misuse Control: The use of technical aids must not compromise the integrity of the assessment. Aids that are not explicitly allowed or are used to engage in fraudulent activities (such as accessing unauthorised information or communication) are prohibited. Violations will be considered academic misconduct and may lead to sanctions according to the examination regulations.

Technical Support: If technical issues arise during the assessment (e.g., software errors or device malfunctions), learners must immediately report this to the technical support staff, who will attempt to resolve the issue. If the problem cannot be fixed, an alternative assessment solution may be provided.

Policy for Online Assessments: Specific guidelines about permissible technologies and resources during assessments are provided before each assessment. These guidelines are designed to maintain academic integrity while allowing learners to utilise tools relevant to their studies and professional fields.

### Innovative Development

The institute recognises the importance of embracing innovative developments in assessment practices to enhance learning outcomes and ensure that assessments remain relevant in a rapidly changing educational landscape. This includes the integration of new technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), adaptive learning tools, and digital platforms that support more personalised and efficient assessment processes. While we encourage the use of these innovations to improve both learner engagement and the accuracy of evaluations, it is essential that their implementation aligns with the university's core values of fairness, transparency, and academic integrity. ### Assessment Structure - Programmatic Assessment Model

#### What is Programmatic Assessment?

Programmatic assessment is a system in which multiple low, medium, and high-stake assessments are distributed across the programme to support continuous learning, formative feedback, and holistic evaluation of learner competencies. Instead of relying on isolated exams, learning is assessed through a range of evidence-gathering moments integrated into learning.

#### Build-Up of Assessments

The learning and assessment structure is built around three stake levels, culminating in a high-stake VIVA and portfolio review by a panel at the end of a certification:

Low-Stake Assessments: Examples include workshop exercises, weekly tasks, peer/self assessments, learning reflections. Their main purpose is to drive learning based on feedback and feedforward.

Medium-Stake Assessments: Examples include end-of-module projects, end-of-certification projects.

High-Stake Assessments: Examples include Certification VIVAs\*, portfolio reviews by a panel, and the final project.

\*VIVAs take place individually with learners and a panel as the final decision-making moment at the end of a certification in the programme.

Also, the assessment of the programme ensures the following:

Alignment with Intended Learning Outcomes: Assessments are directly tied to the programme’s competency-based learning outcomes.

Diversity of Methods: This includes various methods such as hands-on projects, reflective accounts, or presentations to accommodate various learning preferences.

Certifications: A sufficient amount of learning evidence is required to pass each certification, which is made up of several modules, as outlined in the TER. The final decision on the student’s progression to the next certification is based on the substantiated portfolio evidence for the learning outcomes in the certification.

The quality and alignment of these assessments are reviewed cyclically by both the curriculum team and independently by the Examination Board.

Quality Criteria for the Datapoints

For each of the competencies, the data points and evidence are mapped. Each datapoint mapped to a competence must satisfy the assessment criteria or standards associated with that competence.  
 This means:

The evidence is valid (relevant and aligned to the competence).

The evidence is reliable (not contradictory, clearly traceable, consistent)

The evidence is sufficient for judging the specific learning outcome.

The evidence is authentic (owned by the learner, original, with proper referencing)

Basic starting points are that medium stake data points are always signed off by an official representative of the organisation. High stake decisions are taken by a panel of examiners.

Assessment Cycle

In addition to standard procedures stated in the institutional level policy section, the following cycle steps apply especially to the Professional Master's in Applied AI programme.

### Design of Assessment

Assessments in the Professional Master's in Applied Artificial Intelligence are designed as evidence-building events that collectively demonstrate the learner’s mastery of programme competencies (applied AI and applied research) over time. The design stage therefore, focuses on creating a balanced, longitudinal set of low, medium, and high-stake tasks.

Industry Relevance: Competencies are aligned with current industry needs and ensure graduates are equipped with practical and applicable skills at the professional master's level.

Practical Learning Evidence: Assessments are based on tasks that contribute to portfolio development and that allow learners to demonstrate their mastery through tangible outcomes.

Rubric-Based Evaluation: Competencies are evaluated using rubrics that provide transparent and objective descriptions of different levels of mastery.

Stake-level mapping & alignment matrix: During design, every task is tagged Low, Medium, or High stake and entered into a living matrix document that links programme competencies → certification LOs → module LOs → assessment tasks. This mapping document makes each task’s role in the feedback loop and progression impact explicit, secures constructive alignment, and exposes any gaps early.

Transparent communication of the assessment methodology: Each module is detailed to provide learners with comprehensive information about: 1) Module content with their target competencies and learning outcomes, 2) Descriptions of each assessment, including the information on the stake-level and its contribution to the portfolio.

This structured approach ensures learners understand how assessments align with their learning and career goals.

#### Development of Assessments

After an assessment concept has been approved in the Design step, assessors who have been officially appointed by the Exam Board complete the following actions to make it ready for learners:

Task-instruction build: Write the clear, student-facing brief: purpose, expected artefact(s), submission type, and timing.

Rubric refinement: Polish the criterion-referenced rubric: descriptors for each performance level are concise, observable, and directly linked to the learning outcomes recorded in the alignment matrix.

Four-eye check: Peer review within the curriculum team checks validity, workload, inclusiveness, and language clarity.

Release of the assessments: Publish the instructions and rubrics in the LMS at least 7 days before the start of a module for necessary four-eye checks within the curriculum team. This also ensures a timely communication of the assessments to the learners.

These four steps keep the development focused, transparent, and fully aligned with the programme’s programmatic-assessment model.

#### Use of Advanced Technology in Assessment Development

Assessors are encouraged to use advanced technologies for assessment development under the following conditions:

Tools such as AI-based question generators must align with university guidelines on fairness and transparency.

Assessors must document the use of such tools to ensure traceability and consistency in assessment design and development.

Technology should support, not replace, the critical role of assessors in creating high-quality assessments.

#### Administering Assessment

Administration aims to deliver every assessment in a consistent, secure, and learner-centred manner while capturing the evidence required for programmatic assessment.

Digital submission & time-stamping: All artefacts (low, medium, and high stake) are submitted through the relevant Canvas assignment, which automatically records date/time and stores the file as portfolio evidence.

Identity & integrity checks: Secure log-ins as well as an ID check at the start of any live VIVA confirm authorship. Any suspected lack of academic integrity is logged and referred to the Examination Board.

Deadlines & extensions: Students are expected to respect the published deadlines. Extension requests, supported by evidence of special circumstances, are submitted via the standard online form for approval.

Assessor responsibilities:

Low- & medium-stake tasks: The assessor checks the submission, applies the rubric, and records feedback in Canvas.  

High-stake tasks (VIVA, portfolio): A three-member panel conducts the session, records it, completes panel rubrics, and enters the final decision on the designated platform.

Data capture & monitoring: Rubric marking and feedback recorded in Canvas feed the platform’s native analytics. That enables curriculum teams to monitor progression patterns regularly.

Secure archiving: All submissions, recordings, rubrics, and panel minutes are retained for the period specified in the TER and in accordance with GDPR.

Role of the Examination Board: The Examination Board oversees compliance with assessment policies, resolves disputes, and handles cases of irregularities or appeals. Learners may appeal within five working days of the result release. The Examination Board reviews all evidence and issues a decision. Learners have a right to appeal for which they are referred to NRTO.

These procedures uphold consistency, fairness, and integrity while ensuring every artefact is securely stored for the continuous programmatic-assessment feedback loop.

The portfolio is the learner’s curated evidence hub. Supporting artefacts, reflections, or performance tasks are tagged to a specific learning outcome (LO) and can be updated as mastery improves.

Progress decisions follow direct progression, conditional-progression and no progression rules regarding the portfolio reviews, and a three-member panel safeguards fairness and validity.

The Use of Feedback

Feedback is a continuous, multi-directional conversation that accompanies every assessment, from quick knowledge checks to the final VIVA. It;

(a) feeds up by clarifying the expected standard,

(b) feeds back by describing current performance, and

(c) feeds-forward by outlining concrete next steps.

Input comes from faculty, peers, and the learner’s own reflections, which ensure that growth signals appear early and often across all stake levels.

We have also identified “Formal feedback” used at medium and high-stake moments to inform progression decisions, and it has the following features:

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Feature | Purpose / Rationale |
| Decision-making trigger | Occurs at medium- and high-stake points, so panels have reliable evidence. |
| Progress validation | Confirms whether the learner is on track for the intended outcomes. |
| Structured with a rubric | Tied directly to learning outcomes, makes feed-up and feed-forward action-oriented. |
| Written and archived | Stored in the LMS for transparency, audit, and longitudinal tracking. |

### Certifications and Diploma

All award decisions are governed by the Teaching & Examination Regulations (TER). Programmatic assessment looks at evidence-based judgments on learning outcomes mastery to determine certifications and diplomas.

#### Certifications

A learner is awarded a certification when the following conditions are met:

Portfolio evidence: All core learning outcomes for that certification are demonstrated at or above the Sufficient rubric level.

VIVA: The certification VIVA panel records a direct progression.

Conditional items cleared: Any remediation actions from a previous conditional progression are successfully evidenced.

Once the panel records Progression, the Programme Manager logs the decision and issues the certification in accordance with the TER.

#### Diploma

To be eligible for the diploma, the learner must:

Pass all three certifications, including clearance of any conditional items.

Complete and defend the Final Project. Evidence set includes:  
\- Project Plan  
\- Final Report including all technical artefacts.  
\- VIVA / QA session

Each component is judged with a rubric; the overall project must reach at least Sufficient on every criterion.

Final VIVA: The Panel records a Pass confirming programme-wide mastery of all learning outcomes.

After the panel’s decision, the Examination Board conducts a final check for TER compliance and formally confers the diploma.

Learners initiate the diploma process by submitting their completed portfolio and Final-Project artefacts via Canvas; approvals and award notifications follow the TER timelines.

#### Quality Assurance

Quality Assurance Cycle: Team Academy uses a "Plan-Do-Act-Check" cycle to ensure continuous improvement. This involves curriculum evaluations after each annual implementation and assessments at the programme level. Feedback is gathered internally and externally from learners, faculty, and field representatives through surveys or formal meetings. This feedback is then used to inform changes for future cohorts.

Examination Board Oversight: The Examination Board is responsible for ensuring the quality of examinations and exit qualifications. They establish standards for evaluations and awarding results.
