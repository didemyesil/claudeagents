\- to launch a new AI & Data Science product/model (e.g job vacancy scraper);

\- to enquire into a particular area with a view to developing it;

\- to introduce a new technical procedure;

**Methodology**

In order to progress towards a successful outcome of the project, we employ a method called **Action Research** as a research methodology. This methodology, with which you become familiar during your Leadership modules, develops knowledge through being and doing. In other words, it is grounded in your own lived experience aiming to develop a living theory of practice development. This is done by asking a fundamental but profound  question: “How can I improve my practice?”  As an action researcher, you will be able to create an educational influence in your own learning, in the learning of others and in the learning of the social formation in which you live and work.

*“Action research is a situated activity that locates the observer in the world.  \[It\] consists of a set of interpretive, material practices that make the world visible.  These practices transform the world.  They turn the world into a series of representations, including field notes, interviews, conversations, photographs, recordings, and memos to the self. This means that qualitative researchers study things in their natural settings, attempting to make sense of or interpret phenomena in terms of the meanings people bring to them.”*

  
  

**Action research: How does it work?** 

This research methodology proposes an iterative research cycle as below. 

*Constructing*  What is my inquiry, and why does it matter?

*Planning action* With whom, with what, and how will I inquire?

*Taking action*  What happens when I put my plans into action?

*Evaluating action* To what extent are the outcomes in line with my intention, my plan and my data? What more needs to happen?

  
  

Because of its action research set-up, the final project is conducted in two cycles. 

Once the project activities in cycle 1 are completed, you review their previous actions, 

reflect on them and improve them in cycle 2.

  
  
  

**Action Research Basic Recipe**

1.  Articulate the action research question   :   How can I…?    …in relation to these issues that present for me and others in this situation, within this context and culture, for whom it matters because…
2.  Develop a stakeholder network map  :   …of those who affect or are affected by your action research. Who needs to be involved?  Who has influence in the system?
3.  Design a participative process  :   …that is clear about the degree of participation appropriate at each stage from articulating the research question to evaluating the results
4.  Consider what evidence will be needed  :   …as quantitative data and qualitative experiences to provide indicators of improvement and achievement
5.  Plan to analyze data and incorporate stakeholder perspectives as findings  :   …in discussions on outcomes in relation to the intended achievement and its benefits
6.  Develop an action plan  :   …with those who are able to make the research happen
7.  Reflect on learning  :   ..to evaluate the personal, collective and systemic development experiences from participating in the action research

  

**Achieving Quality in Action Research**

In the context of the Professional Master’s in Applied AI, achieving quality in Action Research (AR) demands more than methodological rigour - it calls for conscious, reflective engagement with real-world systems and the humans within them. Quality in Action Research is not measured solely by control or predictability, but by the **transformative value** it brings to practice, learning, and the stakeholders involved. Applied AI researchers are uniquely positioned to bridge technical innovation with meaningful social impact. This requires holding space for uncertainty, co-creating knowledge with users, and treating each intervention as a learning moment.

Action research quality is enhanced through four critical dimensions: **practical relevance**, **process transparency**, **methodological integrity**, and **participative reflexivity**. First, your project must address a genuine need or concern within your professional context - this is what gives applied research its power. Whether you're redesigning a chatbot to better serve vulnerable users or refining an internal AI-driven workflow, your inquiry should be driven by a felt sense of purpose and contribution. Second, your process should be visible and traceable. That includes maintaining documentation of iterations, capturing stakeholder voices, and reflecting on how choices were made. A project journal is not a formality; it is your laboratory notebook and ethical compass.

Third, methodological integrity in action research is about alignment—between your research question, intervention, data, and reflections. Unlike traditional research, Action Research embraces cycles: plan–act–observe–reflect. But within those cycles, your use of AI tools, data pipelines, or models must be purposeful and situated in context. A great action research project doesn’t aim for generalization, but for **situated validity**—deep, local insight that sparks informed change. Whether you use surveys, interviews, system logs, or co-design workshops, your methods should be adapted to the problem and thoughtfully justified.

Fourth and most powerfully, reflexivity: quality in action research comes from a stance of **researching with, not on** people. That means involving stakeholders not only as data sources but as co-researchers. You must regularly examine your own assumptions, values, and role in the system—especially as a technologist. Are you reinforcing bias? Are you empowering or marginalizing certain voices? In action research, your subjectivity is not a flaw—it’s part of the data. The more transparent and reflexive you are, the more robust your project will be.

Finally, quality in action research comes from **ethical clarity and iterative humility**. Applied AI systems evolve. So must your research. Each cycle of action research is an opportunity to learn, recalibrate, and share. Your final thesis should tell the story of that learning—not just what worked, but what didn’t and why. That is where quality lives: in the courage to explore, the discipline to reflect, and the commitment to improve both your practice and the system around you.

By embedding these principles, you will not only achieve the professional master’s standards but also  your applied research project can become a powerful vehicle for responsible AI practice, meaningful impact, and your own transformation as a practitioner-scholar.

**Academic  Integrity and the Use of Advanced Technologies (Including AI)**

As a learner in the *Professional Master's in Applied AI*, you are expected to uphold the highest standards of academic integrity. This includes being transparent about the tools and technologies you use in your work.

You must submit a signed declaration with your Final Project that includes the following:

  - A statement confirming that the work is your own, and that all sources and contributions have been properly acknowledged.  
      
  - A clear explanation of any Advanced Technologies used during your project, including but not limited to:  
      
      
      - Large Language Models (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini)
      - Code generation tools (e.g., GitHub Copilot)
      - AI-based data analysis tools
      - Pre-trained models or APIs  
          
  - You are expected to reflect on the extent and purpose of these tools in your work (e.g. for inspiration, debugging, or data preprocessing) and how they align with ethical and professional standards.  
      

These tools may support your learning and project execution, but they must not replace your own critical thinking, analysis, or authorship.

If you fail to disclose the use of such technologies, or if you present AI-generated work as your own without transparency, your work will be considered as a violation of academic integrity. For further information please see the Guidelines on the *Use of Advanced Technologies in your Studies.*

  

# **4. ACTION RESEARCH CYCLES and STEPS OF THE PROJECT**

**The Cycles and Steps**

The action research methodology is introduced at the start of the programme and spread out to the leadership modules. These modules integrate action research methodology with leadership practice. While you are learning about the methodology, you construct your project idea, plan your actions, present your project plan and get it approved by the organization and your supervisor. In the following section, you will find more information as to each project step. **The project supervisor is expected to** **evaluate progress at the completion of each step** **so you can be made aware of any shortcomings before you move further in their project.** 

**Pre-step: Understand Context & Rationale.** Two main questions: 

1.  *What is the rationale for action?*  Why is this project necessary or desirable (especially also for the learner as an individual), and start to make sense of the economic, social or technical forces driving the need for action.
2.  *What is the rationale for research?* This involves asking how action research is an appropriate methodology to adopt and what contribution it is expected to make.

**Cycle 1, Step 1:  Constructing the Project Proposal**

This is the start of defining the project problem that you want to have an inquiry about and bring a solution to, within your context, by combining your action research and AI mindset. 

Purpose: define a meaningful problem situated in your real context, understand who is involved, and ground your inquiry in Action Research and professional practice. At this stage, you **explore**, map the terrain, and establish relevance without yet interrogating yourself deeply.

**Required outputs**

  - Draft problem definition and intended impact
  - Stakeholder/context map  
    Review of Action Research and relevant professional/technical literature
  - Initial scope and rationale
  - Initial reflective notes (observations only)  
      

**Core activities**

  - Explore your organisational / professional context and identify a concrete opportunity or challenge
  - Map stakeholders and their roles, interests, and relevance
  - Review Action Research literature and relevant sector/technical sources to situate your thinking  
    Clarify the practical outcome you aim to move toward (solution intention, not design yet)
  - Begin reflective journaling: early observations, not yet deep 1st-person inquiry

**Guiding questions**

  - What is happening in my context that requires improvement or innovation?
  - Who is involved or affected by this challenge?
  - What existing literature and professional knowledge relate to this issue?
  - What outcome or change am I aiming for in practical terms?
  - What early observations arise as I start exploring this topic?

**Checklist for a well-constructed problem:**

  - Addresses a real need in a real context
  - Situated in personal motivation and professional purpose
  - Clear, focused, and actionable
  - Informed by initial research and comparable solutions
  - Connects technical goals with leadership inquiry goals
  - Feasible within time and data constraints
  - States success criteria and boundaries

Defining a research problem can be challenging. To make things easier, you are provided with a checklist for a “good research/project problem” (Appendix IV). 

  

**Cycle 1, Step 2: Planning Action**

Purpose: turn your proposal into a concrete, ethical, and feasible plan while now engaging intentionally in 1st-person inquiry. The focus is to challenge your assumptions, set clear methods, and plan how you will build, validate, and learn.

**Required outputs**

  - Final project proposal submitted and reviewed
  - Detailed project plan approved (green light)
  - Reflective journal showing surfaced assumptions and leadership intent

**Core activities**

  - Surface and challenge assumptions about the problem, users, feasibility, and expected outcomes
  - Frame your research and solution question precisely
  - Define data sources, methods, and ethical requirements
  - Plan technical approach (algorithms, tools, evaluation metrics)
  - Plan end-product format (dashboard, model, API, automation, etc.) and initial sketches
  - Identify contributors and access points inside the organisation or context
  - Map your skills vs required skills and learning plan
  - Establish timeline and resource plan
  - Reflect on leadership stance and insider-researcher role

**Guiding questions**

  - How does my organisational context shape this work and create constraints or opportunities?
  - What assumptions must I test?
  - What do I want to achieve  technically and developmentally?
  - What data will I collect and how will I access it responsibly?
  - What methods and evaluation metrics will I use and why are they appropriate?
  - What will my end product look like, and how will I prototype early (wireframes / sketches)?
  - Who do I need to involve and how will I engage them?
  - What skills do I need to stretch, and how will I develop them in this project?
  - How will I ensure fairness, transparency, privacy, and ethical compliance in my solution?
  - What am I learning about myself, my role, and my impact as I begin planning?

  
  
  
  

**Cycle 1, Step 3: Taking Action**

At this stage, you advance from concept to a working prototype while validating assumptions through **2nd-person inquiry** and integrating feedback into the project plan.

Required outputs of this stage:

  - First functional prototype or data product/dashboard
  - Evidence of 2nd-person inquiry (peer, stakeholder, supervisor dialogues)
  - Reflection on insights gained and changes made
  - Updated project plan incorporating feedback

Core activities of this stage:

  - Prototype the solution or dashboard to a usable, testable state aligned with the initial intent.
  - Conduct structured 2nd-person inquiry to validate scope, assumptions, and value:  
     • 1–1 dialogue with a relevant stakeholder or expert  
     • Small-group peer or user discussion
  - Analyse feedback to extract insights on both the technical solution and interpersonal/leadership dimension.
  - Update the project plan based on validated learning and revised understanding of the problem, user needs, and impact path.
  - Track how your leadership, inquiry depth, and problem-solving approaches are evolving as you take action and engage others.

Guiding questions of this stage:

  - What does my first prototype concretely demonstrate?
  - What did I learn about my solution from 2nd-person inquiry?
  - What assumptions were challenged? What validated learning emerged?
  - What adaptations did I make to my plan and why?
  - How am I showing curiosity and influence in stakeholder conversations?
  - What am I learning about myself, the value I create, and the impact I aim to make?

**Cycle 2, Step 1: Building the Solution**

Purpose: translate validated insights from 2nd-person inquiry into a structured, functioning solution. This stage is execution-focused: implement, code, model, and configure the system based on what has been learned in the previous cycle and stage.

**Required outputs**

  - Working model/system or expanded prototype
  - Documented design and implementation decisions tied to 2nd-person insights
  - Versioned artefacts (code, pipeline, architecture notes, data transformations)
  - Rationale for chosen methods, models, and tools when alternatives exist

**Core activities**

  - Operationalise the revised plan: implement the solution grounded in stakeholder feedback and clarified scope.
  - Build core components:  
     • data pipelines and preprocessing  
     • model(s) or algorithmic logic  
     • dashboards, interfaces, or automation workflows
  - Apply insights gained from stakeholder dialogues to prioritise features, refine workflows, and tailor the system to context.
  - Document architecture, design logic, and trade-offs as you build.
  - Maintain alignment with the original research question, intended impact, and leadership inquiry focus.

**Guiding questions**

  - How does this build reflect what I learned from 2nd-person inquiry?
  - What design or modelling decisions am I making and why?
  - What trade-offs am I accepting and how do I justify them?
  - How am I demonstrating disciplined execution, not drifting from problem scope?
  - What leadership habits/behaviors am I applying here (persistence, clarity, accountability, curiosity.

**Cycle 2, Step 2: Reviewing & Evaluating Action** 

Purpose: evaluate the solution through 3rd-person inquiry. Move beyond personal interpretation and stakeholder dialogue to gather structured, external evidence on its effectiveness, organisational relevance, and impact. This phase focuses on testing assumptions, validating outcomes, and identifying improvements grounded in objective feedback and observable results.

**Expected outputs**

  - Documented 3rd-person inquiry evidence (evaluation data, structured feedback, usability results, performance observations)
  - Analysis of outcomes against original objectives and success criteria
  - Identification of improvement areas and next steps
  - Reflection on leadership development and inquiry stance

**Core activities**

  - Apply 3rd-person inquiry: gather objective data and structured external input (e.g. user testing, expert evaluation, observational evidence, performance metrics, feedback instruments).
  - Examine how the solution performs in the real organisational or stakeholder context.
  - Compare results to initial goals, scope, and assumptions.
  - Identify gaps, constraints, and misalignments revealed by evidence.
  - Understand organisational consequences: value created, friction encountered, readiness, adoption barriers, ethical considerations.
  - Reflect on personal leadership behaviours in this evaluation phase.

Guiding questions

  - What objective evidence do I have about how the solution performs and is perceived?
  - How do the results align with my intended outcomes and success criteria?
  - What failed, what worked, and what did I not anticipate?
  - What organisational value or challenges emerged?
  - Which assumptions were confirmed or disproven by external evidence?
  - What improvements are necessary before the next iteration?
  - How did organisational dynamics and power relationships influence feedback, adoption, or resistance?
  - What leadership patterns am I demonstrating in how I evaluate, respond to evidence, and plan forward?

Cycle 2, Step 3: Finalise the End Product and Complete the Project Report

Purpose: finalise the technical solution, complete the full Action Research project report, and deliver outcomes to the organisation. This stage integrates all inquiry cycles and formalises learning, impact, and professional growth.

**Required outputs**

  - Finalised solution for your context/organisation
  - Full, complete project report with all required chapters (see Appendix VII)

**Core activities**

  - Refine and polish the solution based on evaluation findings and organisational context.
  - Complete the entire project report, ensuring all chapters (Appendix VII) are addressed coherently.
  - Synthesize inquiry and technical evidence to demonstrate rigour and progression.
  - Deliver a structured presentation to your organisation about your solution

**Guiding questions**

  - How does the full report demonstrate both technical mastery and Action Research discipline?
  - In what ways did each inquiry lens (1st, 2nd, 3rd person) shape the project?
  - What change - however small - did I influence in practice, and how do I evidence it?
  - How did my leadership and inquiry stance evolve across the cycles?
  - How do I articulate the value created, lessons learned, and future direction?

**Assessment of the Final Project** 

**Evidence set includes:**

  - *\*a project plan,\**
  - *\*a final report,\** 
  - *\*A Final VIVA presentation + QA.\**

Each component is judged with a rubric; it must reach at least **Sufficient** on every criterion.

Your final VIVA will be assessed by a **panel** composed of:

  - **Three members (including one independent faculty member)**, forming a **four-eye principle** evaluation panel.  
      
  - These members will represent **domain expertise** in:  
      
      
      - Applied AI
      - Applied Research

This structure ensures that both the technical, leadership and action research dimensions of your work are thoroughly and fairly evaluated.

# **5. SUPERVISION PROCESS** 

Supervision ensures that your final project develops with methodological and technical rigor, ethical discipline, and real-world relevance. It supports your inquiry process, challenges your assumptions, and keeps you accountable to your plan while you have full responsibility for your decisions, execution, and the quality of your work. Your supervisor guides the *process*, not the content, and does not do the work for you.

  

Supervision is structured across each Action Research step. You may revise your plan as the project evolves, but changes must be discussed and agreed with your supervisor to ensure coherence and feasibility.

### **When Supervision Begins**

**In the part-time** version of the programme, supervision begins once you complete the three certificates and submit your initial Project Proposal. You then work with your supervisor to refine this into a full Project Plan before beginning Cycle 1.

**In the full-time** version, supervision begins from the start of the programme. As you learn the Action Research methodology and leadership practices in early modules, you meet regularly with your supervisor to shape your inquiry focus, test early assumptions, and gradually develop your proposal and plan alongside your learning. The supervision rhythm is embedded into your study from the beginning rather than concentrated at the end.

In both pathways, the purpose is the same: to help you build clarity, discipline, and responsible practice from the moment your inquiry takes shape.

### **Role of the Supervisors**

You have separate applied AI and applied research supervisors. They support the *integrity of your process*. They:

  - challenge your reasoning, assumptions, and inquiry approach
  - help you stay focused and realistic
  - question feasibility, ethical considerations, and responsible-AI decisions
  - ensure your project aligns with Action Research cycles and academic standards
  - advise on selecting literature, inquiry frameworks, and methods
  - test the logic behind your data, model choices, and evaluation strategies
  - guide you in critically reflecting on leadership, power dynamics, and professional impact
  - approve each stage before you move on

Their role is not to design your model, write your code, or solve technical problems for you- those remain your responsibility.

### **Your Role as the Learner**

You lead the work. You are expected to:

  - come prepared to each meeting with clear updates and artefacts
  - bring decisions, not just questions
  - apply feedback and show visible progression
  - document discussions, decisions, milestones, and reflections
  - demonstrate independence in technical and inquiry decisions
  - manage time and planning with discipline
  - uphold ethical and responsible-AI standards
  - seek support early if barriers appear

Supervision is an active partnership; its effectiveness depends on your preparation and engagement.

### **Review of the Project Plan** 

The first meeting with your supervisor is to review the draft Project Plan which should be based on the already approved Project Proposal. The supervisor needs to establish if and how the proposed project needs to be further fine-tuned. The check-box mentioned in Appendix IV can also be used in this step. Are the objectives clear, do they make sense, are they of value? Is the project feasible? Do you have access to the required information and data? Are you able to carry out the task? Furthermore, the planning needs to be reviewed. Is it feasible? Adjustments should be made to fit both you and the supervisor’s schedule. 

### **Planning** 

The final project is a huge undertaking for you as a learner and it requires great discipline and motivation to keep focused. A clear planning will help you keep the project moving along. The planning is not fixed in stone and can be adjusted. A supervisor progress report per project step is also included in the planning table. Your supervisors must provide short comments on how well (or badly) the project is progressing. 

  

To help with the planning, we require you to record the completion of important milestones. The Planning Card in Appendix III shows the main milestones that you need to complete, and you need to include these in the Project Plan. You must write down a date when you expect to complete the milestone. Once it is completed (and this requires acceptance from the supervisor), you should also add that date to the table.

### **Feedback from your Supervisor**

Besides your own personal efforts, it is also essential that you get clear and effective feedback from your supervisor at every step***.*** Supervisors tasks are as follows: 

  - To question AI and research choices and thus force you to re-evaluate them;
  - To provide focus and feedback;
  - To discuss the planning;
  - To advise on the choice of literature; 
  - To advise on the research methods and data collection tools; 
  - To ensure the paper meets academic standards;
  - To sign off on the final version of the final project report before it gets submitted.

## **Appendix IV: Project Proposal**

When starting to consider a final project, two questions should first be addressed: 

1.  *What is the rationale for action?*  Why is this project necessary or desirable (especially also for the learner as an individual), and start to make sense of the economic, social or technical forces driving the need for action.
2.  *What is the rationale for research?* This involves asking how action research is an appropriate methodology to adopt and what contribution it is expected to make.

  

This is the start of defining the problem that the learner wants to have an inquiry about and bring a solution to, within their context, by combining their action research and data science mindset. The next question asked at this stage is “What do I want to achieve and/or improve?”. 

  

The **project proposal** should include a literature review of different or partial AI/DS and applied research approaches to solve the same problem. Besides, some preliminary desktop research that they have done on the topic can be integrated. The proposal should make them think through exactly what it is that they are going to do, and reflect on the leadership questions that need to be addressed at this stage.
