# Faculty Professionalisation Practice — Bachelor's in Applied Computing

### A Dynamic, Inquiry-Driven Approach to Professional Development

## Overview

Faculty development is valued because it strengthens collective capacity for impactful leadership, growth, and learner achievement, in line with the Leadership Manifesto (responsibility, collaboration, shared leadership). Faculty development is not a one-time event but a continuous, reflective, action-based process strengthening the collective ability to design impactful learning experiences, implement meaningful assessments, and facilitate student engagement.

Designed to be: **Inquiry-driven** (experiment, reflect, iterate); **Collaborative** (shared leadership and collective learning); **Adaptable** (faculty-driven, aligned with institutional goals). Empowers faculty as reflective practitioners, adaptive educators, and proactive professionals in curriculum design, assessment & feedback, and facilitative teaching.

## Intended Outcomes

Faculty are encouraged to: enhance reflective practice; improve target-competency practices (e.g. assessment, effective feedback); experiment with methodologies/tools; strengthen collaboration and shared leadership via faculty learning communities.

## The Professional Development Cycle

Cyclical and non-linear, with structured reflection, experimentation, and group learning at each phase; each step facilitated once per group by an experienced facilitator; can be adapted/shortened.

### 1. Strategic Intent & Outcomes: Setting the Foundation

Align individual professional goals with institutional priorities. Key questions: What kind of educator do I aspire to be? How can I enhance student learning through curriculum design, assessment, or facilitation? Where do I need to grow? Practice: self-assess current strategies; define focus areas; set strategic learning goals connected to student learning outcomes; use peer mentoring to refine goals.

### 2. Inquiry as a Dynamic Process: Experimenting & Learning

Test new teaching/assessment methods through inquiry-driven learning. Key questions: What changes can I experiment with? What can I learn further? How can I integrate methods/tools effectively? How do students respond? Practice: select a specific challenge/opportunity; engage in micro-experiments (AI-assisted feedback tools, new rubrics, alternative instructional methods); take an online course/MOOC; capture observations via teaching logs/faculty discussion groups.

### 3. Practice in Action: Implementing & Observing Change

Apply/analyse new strategies in real-world teaching. Key questions: How are students responding to changes? Are methods improving engagement/learning? What adjustments are needed? Practice: implement changes; use student engagement data/peer observations/self-reflection to measure impact; observe behaviours and unintended consequences; adjust in real time.

### 4. Reflection & Learning: Analyzing Impact & Adjusting Strategies

Evaluate outcomes and refine approaches. Key questions: What worked? What didn't? What insights apply to future teaching? How can I share learning with colleagues? Practice: analyse feedback/peer insight data; structured self-reflection; identify areas for adjustment/further experimentation.

### 5. Emergent Learning & Opportunities: Scaling & Sustaining Growth

Move from personal practice to collective faculty development. Key questions: How can I contribute to faculty learning/leadership? What long-term innovations can we implement as a team? Practice: mentor new colleagues; engage in collaborative projects (e.g. designing new assessment methods for the AI-driven world); lead workshops to share innovative practices; establish sustained faculty inquiry groups.
